About

I've always been interested in what design can do when it stops looking backward.

I studied graphic design at Harrington College of Design in Chicago, then spent years working in spaces that hadn't fully figured out what design even meant yet — game UX, the creator economy, Web3. The work was harder, more ambiguous, more interesting. I kept going toward the edges.

Somewhere in the middle, I got certified in additive manufacturing at MIT. Not because I planned to become an engineer, but because understanding how physical things are made changes how you think about all systems design. Constraints reveal structure.

I'm currently founding Curmunchkins — an edtech platform designed from the ground up for neurodivergent kids. It's the most challenging design problem I've worked on, because the users are kids who've been failed by "one-size-fits-all" for their entire lives. Getting it right matters.

In parallel, I build tools. Tessello — a parametric pattern generator — came out of a Figma Makeathon and reflects a simple belief: designers who make their own tools see differently.

Thinking

01

Sensory-first isn't accessibility — it's better design for everyone.

When you design for the most demanding user, you remove friction for everyone else. The features that make products usable for kids with ADHD or sensory processing differences — clear hierarchy, predictable pacing, reduced noise — make every product better. We've had it backwards: accessibility isn't accommodation, it's the standard.

02

A designer who builds tools sees design problems differently.

Tessello didn't exist because a brief asked for it. It existed because the existing tools were wrong. Designers who build their own tools have necessarily understood the problem deeply enough to notice what's missing. That's a different relationship to design problems than most people in the field have.

03

Every screen in a game is a contract. Break it once and you lose the player forever.

Game UX trained a kind of precision that's rare in product design. Players form expectations at 60fps. There's no asynchronous feedback loop — no survey, no NPS score. Just the immediate, unforgiving judgment of someone who chose to be there. Design for that audience and everything else gets easier.

04

Pattern tools treat patterns as finished things. Tessello treats them as variables.

The entire category of existing pattern tools — Heropatterns, Figma's repeat grid — gives you outputs to choose from. Tessello gives you a parametric engine: a reason to think about what a pattern actually is. The distinction matters whenever you're designing with a tool versus designing the tool itself.

05

The frontier is uncomfortable by design. That's the signal it's the frontier.

Web3 was messy. Game development is brutal. Neurodivergent edtech has no playbook. These aren't bugs. The discomfort is the indicator that the map hasn't been drawn yet — which means there's something there worth figuring out. The designers who build in comfortable spaces build for a market that already exists.
